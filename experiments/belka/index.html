EEG / virtual reality / 2016

Belka

An EEG headset, a virtual world, and a room full of people determined to prove how calm they were.

A Vive, a computer and an idea

In 2016, I found a hackathon called Hack the Senses in London and invited my friend Doniyor. It sounded like a fun thing to do. I brought an HTC Vive and my computer, which became our development studio, and pitched a question: could we build a virtual environment that responded to someone’s brain activity?

People voted for the ideas they wanted to work on. A team formed around mine, and we became Belka. The ambition was to generate complex environments, with music changing alongside them. Doniyor worked on turning the noisy EEG data into a usable signal; other teammates took on the environment, the experience and the soundtrack.

By the last-minute push, the complex world had become an open space that changed colour. The generative music hadn’t come together. That was a difficult problem, and a lot of effort went into trying. The colour changes worked, so that was what we had to build an experience around.

There were two people in the experiment. One wore the EEG sensor; the other wore the Vive and looked around a world responding to the first person’s signal. Sitting someone down with a sensor on their head didn’t give us much to look at. I edited a short video that moved between calming and irritating images and music. We also brewed coffee and brought it over for the smell. Those stimuli gave the EEG wearer something to respond to, and the person inside the headset something to notice.

We won the Intel Code for Good prize and came second at the hackathon. I was excited about carrying on, but the core development team didn’t want to continue. We had made a working prototype together. For most of the team, that was where it ended.

The calmness competition

Part of the prize was an invitation to exhibit at The Tech Expo in London that October. I managed to forget about it until a call roughly two days beforehand: were we coming? Yes, apparently we were. I printed a banner and turned up with an assistant who hadn’t been part of the original team.

I remember a room of perhaps twenty or twenty-five stands, a lot of fintech, and VR companies making virtual property tours for developers selling London apartments. Then there was me in a colourful scarf with an improvised booth and a world that changed colour. I had no business plan to explain.

We put the virtual scene on a plasma screen so everyone could see it. One visitor could wear the EEG; another could enter the world through the Vive. We explained the colours: blue meant calm in our mapping; yellow and red meant less calm.

People started volunteering their composure. “I’m very calm.” All right, put this on. The world would go yellow or red. They would try harder to relax. Their friend would laugh. Now they had an audience, and something to prove.

I enjoyed this and encouraged it. I would explain that lots of people found it difficult to be calm, which was apparently an irresistible invitation to demonstrate that they were different. The same scene kept repeating, with a bigger audience each time. By the last demonstration I remember, perhaps ten or fifteen people were gathered around a man in a suit, watching him try to hold it together while the colours kept moving the wrong way.

Visitors kept asking what I planned to do with it. “I don’t know. Probably nothing.” That seemed to surprise them almost as much as the demonstration. I was interested in the experiment. I didn’t particularly want a startup.

“I could, but I won’t.”

One woman stayed to ask more questions. Could I get a sense of someone’s emotional state using something less obtrusive than an EEG headset? I had been reading about sensing and wearables, going to talks, following the connection between people and machines out of curiosity. I told her I thought it could be done, especially in a controlled environment.

Then she asked whether I would build something like that for Sberbank.

“I could, but I won’t.”

It was a conversation at a stand; we never got as far as a brief or a commission. But I carried that answer around for years, very pleased with myself. What I thought I was refusing, and why I might answer differently now, became the part of Belka that stayed with me longest.

Looking back The audience, my own hubris, and the Sberbank answer I would probably change.

How little was enough

We had imagined a much richer world. Under the pressure of a hackathon, we ended up with colours. At the conference, that turned out to be enough to get people involved. Once they understood the rules, they could watch a change, interpret it and respond to the person wearing the sensor. They didn’t need a complicated environment to care about what happened next.

That was the result I found exciting: how little information people needed to start making sense of one another. There was a lot of room for interpretation in those colours. We hadn’t established that the prototype could reliably read someone’s emotions. But we had made a shared language that people could pick up almost immediately.

The crowd became part of the experiment. Being watched, trying to appear calm, hearing people laugh: all of that changed the situation the person was responding to. I was part of it too. I liked provoking confident fintech people into putting themselves on the spot. Looking back, I can’t describe it as though I simply stood there observing a neutral demonstration. I was helping create the pressure.

It became an experiment with hubris, including mine. They wanted to prove they could control themselves. I enjoyed being the person who could make that difficult. That social scene was much more interesting to me than the product I was supposed to be pitching.

The answer I was proud of

I had left finance a year or two earlier and still had a substantial chip on my shoulder. Sberbank was an especially easy name to refuse: a bank associated with the Russian state, with everything that meant to me and my family.

In my head, I immediately saw a private banking office. A wealthy client comes in; a system reads their reactions; the person selling to them adjusts the script. That was the application I imagined. The woman hadn’t given me that specification. But it was enough for me to feel that helping build it would cross a boundary: taking something personal and using it to sell more effectively.

I felt righteous saying no. For years I told the story as though the answer was obvious. Of course I wouldn’t help them do that. The refusal also fitted very neatly with who I wanted to be after leaving finance.

The answer I would give now

Almost ten years later, I would probably say yes. I still dislike the use I imagined, and I have mixed feelings about that change in myself. But I can also see how much I might have learned by trying to build something difficult. That experience matters to me more now than it did then. I no longer automatically experience the prospect of doing that work as selling out.

My sense of the boundary has changed too. I pay more attention now to how much people already pick up from one another: tension, hesitation, discomfort, confidence. Sensing those things with a machine no longer feels as categorically different to me as it did. I also suspect that comparable systems have been built since, so I wouldn’t feel I was opening the door for the first time.

I don’t think noticing someone’s reaction settles the question of what you should do with it. Reading another person in a room and building a system for a bank still leave different questions about purpose and power. I haven’t resolved those by becoming more curious about the engineering.

I don’t regret the refusal. I also don’t feel the same certainty about it. At the time, saying no gave me a very satisfying ending to the story. Now I’m more interested in the part I never built.
