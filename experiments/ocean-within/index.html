Hybrid documentary / collaboration / 2016–2026

The Ocean Within

In 2016, some friends and I decided to make a film from the perspective of the ocean. I thought we would figure out how along the way. Ten years later, we have a film, and I have considerably more respect for that question.

66:42 · UK / Ukraine · Completed June 2026

The film

The Ocean Within follows marine biologist Alex Semenov between his underwater world and his life on land. Alex and his real-life partner Maya play versions of themselves. Observation of White Sea marine life meets a constructed story, and creatures drift into places where they have no business being. The film moves between what we recorded and what we imagined.

The current version was completed in June 2026. We are seeking festival screenings.

How we got here

I knew Alex before there was a film. He was trying to organise a three-year expedition around the world, and I helped him set up a company for it while I was still working in finance. The expedition never happened. A few years later, in Mexico with friends, I started talking about him, and somebody's documentary idea became this much stranger proposition: could the ocean itself have a point of view?

We shot at the White Sea biological station in autumn 2016, returned for the ice and snow in February, then committed to a much larger production later in 2017. I funded the first two expeditions. For the next one, we found an investor, although filming was already underway by the time the money came through. That should give you some idea of how I was approaching producing.

We had beautiful footage and a film that kept changing shape. Our attempt to write it into a genre movie gave us a villain and twists and a great deal of work. After a long edit in Los Angeles, we managed a version for Venice in 2018. It wasn't accepted. More painfully, I didn't think it worked either. Eventually we stopped.

I returned to it in Kyiv with editor Alexander Chorny. He watched the cut and told me we had three films stitched together. I suspected he might be the person to help us. We set the existing edit aside, went back to the whiteboard and started again. Eventually there was a cut we both recognised as a film. I still remember watching it in his office in Rzhyshchev. Rory later watched that version in London and loved it too.

We hoped to release it in 2022. That didn't happen, and it went on ice again. In Medellín in 2026, conversations with people in film brought me back to a subject I had started describing in the past tense. I revisited the edit, changed the title and, with help, developed a strategy for the hybrid documentary it had become. That is where we are now.

Looking back Momentum, five days a month, and a film still finding its audience.

How hard can it be

When the guys first told me a feature might cost a million dollars, I thought, only a million? I had come from finance. Raising that seemed like a manageable problem. When they said we needed a proper script, I pointed out that people made documentaries and found stories in them all the time. How hard could it be?

Quite hard, as it turns out. I was good at getting things moving. I could find equipment, assemble a crew, organise Arctic clothing and keep a production going through a ridiculous number of logistical problems. I enjoyed that work. But I kept treating movement as a sign that the film was coming together. We could organise another shoot much more readily than we could explain what would make it a film.

Five days a month

Alexander said he would work five days a month, at his full commercial rate, and the film would never be released as a Russian production. I agreed to all three. The schedule initially seemed impossible. We had already spent years on this thing. How could working so little help us finish it?

The space between those days turned out to be useful. We could step back, forget what a scene was supposed to do in the script and look at what was actually there. The film became shorter and much closer to the strange idea we had started with. It was a different kind of work from keeping a crew moving, and it asked something different of me.

Still here

This project gave me an education I could never have planned. It also cost an enormous amount, and I don't expect we will recover the money, although that was always part of the goal. Gratitude doesn't cancel that out. I can value what it gave me and still wish I had made some very different decisions.

It is difficult to call something a failure when you are still trying to give it a life. It is equally difficult to pretend that making a film means everything worked out. For now, I can say we finally have a film I want people to see. What happens when they do is still ahead of us.
