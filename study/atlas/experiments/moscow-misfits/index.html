Production services / collective / 2018–2019 / selected work

Moscow Misfits

We learned how to get a film made in Russia, built a team around it and thought we might turn that experience into a business. The film was draining my money. It seemed reasonable to try making some on the side.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

A production business

Producing Aquatilis, now The Ocean Within, felt like working in the Wild West. Permissions were difficult, and with foreigners in the crew we couldn't afford to improvise our way around them. A shoot being shut down was a real possibility. We learned how to get things done and built a capable team in Russia.

Moscow Misfits was our attempt to offer that team and experience to other productions. I had contacts in China and America and was well established in London. Justin was in Berlin. We thought we could bring international projects into Russia and provide the local production work.

Our website called us a creative laboratory, and there were plenty of creative ambitions in it. But the practical idea was fairly straightforward: we knew people who might need to shoot things, and we knew people who could help them do it. Ideally, some money would change hands.

A difficult place to sell

The problem was finding productions that wanted to take the risk. Russia had already occupied Crimea and was already at war. We weren't as aware of what that meant for our proposition as the people being asked to commit serious production budgets. Visas, permissions and the lack of a convincing rebate proposition made the offer harder still.

The Moscow Film Commission fuck-up

Then we met Ekaterina (Katya) Mtsitouridze, the head of Roskino, and Michel Gomez, who had headed Paris’s film commission. Katya was behind the new Moscow Film Commission initiative, working with the city to bring international productions into Moscow. We met them at Cannes in 2017, where they presented the initiative. That was where our conversations started, and we carried on working with them from there. These were people with actual experience and connections: Katya ran the Russian pavilion at Cannes, and Michel knew how this was supposed to work from doing it in Paris. That mattered. It gave us a reason to take the whole thing seriously. They couldn't believe their luck. Here we were, an international crew that had actually shot in Russia with the permissions in place. We had done it legally, by the book. Apparently, in the conversations they were having, that made us something of a unicorn.

They wanted examples of international productions working in Moscow. We wanted international productions to work in Moscow. They offered to feature us as a production-services agency on their website, and we spent months helping get this thing going. There was an ambitious promotional film, there were plans for a launch, there were people who seemed serious about making it happen. For a while, it looked like the institution we needed was finally being built.

And then they all fucking disappeared. Messages, emails, nothing. We had put work into their project, and for about two months we couldn't even get an explanation of what had happened to it.

The explanation that eventually reached us through contacts in Moscow was almost too Russian to be surprising. There was already an older structure inside the city administration doing, supposedly, this very job. According to what we were told, it had staff, salaries and very little to show for them. A new initiative actually bringing in productions would make that arrangement considerably less comfortable. So instead of helping it, the established operation fought it. And won.

We were also told that the people behind the new initiative ended up spending their remaining resources on a report explaining that the whole thing had been a research project, accounting for the money instead of delivering the launch. I never saw that report or the internal fight. That's the explanation we received afterwards. What I did see was months of our work leading to a wall of silence, with nobody taking responsibility for the partnership they had invited us into.

Later I ran into Michel Gomez at a festival. He was apologetic, and the conversation was painfully awkward. My impression was that he didn't fully understand what had happened either. He had come into this with experience of how a film commission was supposed to work. I had rather more experience of how Russia worked. Neither of us had anything useful to offer the other by then.

This wasn't a minor administrative setback. The whole point of a film commission is to make a difficult place easier to shoot in. Our experience of this initiative was the opposite: another political mess to spend time and money on, another set of promises nobody would answer for. The city still had a film commission. What we no longer had was any reason to trust the opportunity we'd been sold.

FML48 worked

One thing did work: FML48, our forty-eight-hour filmmaking competition. We had practically no budget. I remember spending something like seven dollars testing adverts. We found sponsors, got people involved and ended up with a proper event. Ani and I designed the Chinese-cat awards, we had talks and a ceremony, and people loved it.

I think the important part was giving people a space to actually create together. So much of the film world around us was work, work, work: commercial jobs, then your own passion project squeezed into whatever was left. There weren't many opportunities simply to meet people, try a different role and make something with a new team. FML48 gave people that excuse. It made a community, however briefly. That's a big part of why I'm still so proud of it.

And we didn't just hand out a prize and disappear. The million roubles was development support, and we put a lot of work into developing and pitching the winning team's feature, The Glutton's Will. We wanted to find a route to an international co-production. But the interest we got kept pointing towards production outside Russia. The screenplay was eventually optioned by a Russian producer — I think twice — but never made. That producer didn't need us to provide production services in Russia. We had helped move the project forward, but the business we hoped would follow from it never materialised.

The FML48 page has the films, talks and the fuller story. My contemporary account of what development actually involves is also in Russian writing.

The last proposal

FML48 gave us a reputation as people who could organise something like this. A Russian streaming platform approached us about a nationwide script competition: find the strongest scripts, develop them and produce short-form films. That sounded like a reasonable next step. We put together a proposal, with actual production budgets and fees that would let us get paid for doing the work.

They liked the idea. Getting it commissioned was another matter. There was an internal fight over bringing in an outside production company, and the conversations dragged on. My reading of it was that our proposal put the money in the wrong hands. If the budget went to an outside company responsible for delivering the work, it became much harder for people inside to quietly take their cut. We were proposing a production. They were fighting over control of the budget.

I wasn't sitting in every internal meeting, so that's my interpretation of the resistance we encountered. But it didn't come out of nowhere. At the same time, I was looking for government-backed financing for Aquatilis and being asked, directly, to return half the money as a kickback. Fifty percent. That was the environment in which I was trying to make sense of these conversations.

Eventually the platform offered Ani a job inside the company to run the project. So the idea could apparently go ahead, and someone from our team could do the work, just without Moscow Misfits being hired to produce it. By then I was ready to wind the company down. Ani had put an enormous amount of work into FML48. If she could at least get paid for a few months, fine. I agreed.

She did get a salary for a few months. Something was produced, but it never became the programme we had proposed, and she was taken off it before it finished. We had gone from a commission for our company to a temporary job for one of us, and then even that was over. There wasn't much left to build on.

Other collaborations, elsewhere

And here's the irony. Outside FML48, the other productions we actually managed to deliver through Moscow Misfits didn't happen in Russia at all. They were Sean Rogg's Waldorf Project: FUTURO-X at Wonderfruit in Thailand in December 2018, and FUTURO-A aboard a Lufthansa flight between Germany and the United States in March 2019.

Thailand and the fucking sky. We set up a company to bring international productions into Russia, and the work we managed to produce took us somewhere else entirely. Those projects worked, whatever the difficulties of making them. But they also made it very hard to keep pretending that our original business proposition was going anywhere. That was the final nail.

The FUTURO-X film credits Moscow Misfits with film production. Its making-of gives the individual production roles, including my work as executive producer and drone camera. FUTURO-A credits Justin as producer/camera and me as executive producer.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

The business proposition, the budgets, and what worked anyway.

The business we thought we were building

I thought the difficult part was learning how to produce in Russia. Once we had done that, surely we could offer it to someone else. But knowing how to solve the local problems didn't make the location attractive to the people paying for the production. In hindsight, they understood some of the risks better than I did.

We made Aquatilis with private investors and a willingness to take risks that a conventional large production would have struggled to accept. That was a way of getting our particular film made. It was a poor foundation for assuming we could bring a steady flow of international clients into the same conditions.

What would actually have to change

I don't think you can fix this with a beautiful promotional film and a list of locations. You need permissions people can rely on, workable visa rules, a credible financial reason to come and institutions that care more about getting films made than protecting someone's budget. We were trying to sell a filming destination without those foundations.

The kickback conversations made the problem very concrete for me. Half the money gone before you make anything. What sort of film are you supposed to produce with that? How are you supposed to build a dependable business around it? I didn't want to learn how to operate that way. And the international producers declining to bring their money into Russia weren't lacking imagination. They were being sensible.

There are people making excellent work in Russia despite all this. That doesn't make the system work. My conclusion from trying to build this company was that Russia would never become the filming destination we were pitching unless the policy and the way money was handled changed. We couldn't solve that by being particularly resourceful on set.

A failed business and a good event

Moscow Misfits didn't become what we hoped. It also doesn't make sense to dismiss FML48 because the company around it didn't work out. We brought people together, made an event with very little money and did real development work afterwards. I'm proud of that.

The difference matters to me. A project can work beautifully for the people taking part and still fail to provide the livelihood or continuing business its organisers hoped to build.
