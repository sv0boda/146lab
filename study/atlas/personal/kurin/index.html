Personal / Balyki, Ukraine / 2021–2026

Kurin

A weekend place in Balyki, built around Mykola’s vision. Four months became four years, and finishing it came to mean something very different.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Like a lot of things in my life, this one came out of Ocean Within. While we were editing the film, Alex Chorny invited me to his out-of-town office in Balyki, about an hour and a half or two hours from Kyiv. He and his friend Yura had bought an old Ukrainian clay hut, a mazanka, and spent years turning it into a modern place to live and work.

I came there and completely fell in love. We went for a walk, saw a plot with a for-sale sign, and I wanted to buy it immediately. Alex told me there was a queue. They were trying to be thoughtful about who moved in, and a friend was next in line. I said: fine, give him a month. If he doesn’t buy it, I will.

He didn’t. I did.

A place that already had a life

This was during COVID. My apartment in Kyiv was finished, and I wanted somewhere outdoors to go for weekends. But Balyki wasn’t just an available piece of countryside. It already had something going on.

The houses sit around a yar, a ravine cut into the land by water running toward the Dnipro. Your neighbours might be quite close, but the folds of the ground hide them. Sometimes you see a couple of houses together. Sometimes you see only your own. The landscape gives you this wonderful sense of being alone without actually being isolated.

There were people from film and IT moving out of the city, but they were making an effort to become part of the place. Alex and Yura invited local people to lunches and dinners. They organised tolokas, communal building gatherings, bringing people from town together with the neighbours. It reminded me of the best part of a Burning Man camp. People actually making a life together.

I’d lived in all sorts of temporary homes by then: RVs, trailers, tents, little huts. I loved geodesic domes. But a white dome on this land would have felt completely foreign. I wanted something that belonged here.

Mykola’s idea

That’s where I met Mykola Stepanets. He’d had enough of office life and become a professional woodworker. He and an architect, Jan, had restored another hut nearby. Mykola was also developing an idea with Andrei, an architect and project manager: taking the traditional Ukrainian kurin and making a contemporary version of it.

We talked about it down in the ravine, while washing ourselves at the water source. Neither Alex’s place nor Mykola’s had running water then. You walked a couple of hundred metres down into the yar with your water jugs, filled them, and carried them home. There was another container we used to pour the water over ourselves. The water was fucking cold, whether the day was hot or cold, and I loved it. That walk, filling the jugs, washing there together: it was one of my favourite rituals of being in Balyki.

That’s where Mykola pitched Kurin to me, and where we decided to do it. I could see his vision quite early, even without a set of drawings. I said: I want this. I’ll fund it. Let’s go.

He started with water

One of the first things I did was arrange running water for Kurin.

We dug a shallow well at the bottom of the ravine, a trench up toward the building, and put in the pumps and pipework. I loved going down to the source. I also wanted to be able to turn on a tap.

Later, someone told me that Mykola had been talking about my approach to the project and laughing: “He started with water.” It was affectionate. Here we were working out how to revive this beautiful architectural form, and I was already thinking about the shower. Eventually the other huts got running water too, each in its own way. But I’d started with it.

That probably explains my part in Kurin better than anything else. Mykola was the principal designer and lead artisan. The shape, the visual language, the craftsmanship, the custom-made furniture: that’s his work. I kept obsessing over what it would actually be like to stay there.

A perfect circle, with an addition

The original design was a perfect circle. A beautiful thing. But the longer I spent with the layout, the clearer it became that the circle we’d started with wasn’t going to fit everything comfortably. You could keep moving things around on the plan, but at some point there just wasn’t enough room.

So we added a pristroyka. An extension, technically, although that word doesn’t quite carry the same feeling. A pristroyka is the thing you attach to the beautiful thing because you need more space. I think of those enormous homemade balcony extensions you see in Ukraine, Georgia, Moscow: someone decides the balcony isn’t big enough and builds another fucking room onto it.

We laughed a lot about what we were doing. Mykola had this lovely circular form, and we were attaching an awkward clay addition with a roof that created a whole new set of problems. In my contribution to the architecture, I’d managed to make it less pretty.

But somehow it also felt more authentic. A place changing shape because someone needs to live in it. And the arrangement worked beautifully: the kitchen and entrance became a separate zone from the main space for sleeping and hanging out. In bad weather, that separation really matters.

That was how we worked together. Mykola developed the identity of the place, down to the furniture and the way the materials meet. I kept coming back to the layout, the water, the hot-water boiler, the shower, the everyday comfort. You can stay in this very particular, handmade space and still have the things you expect from a modern home. For me, that combination is what makes Kurin so special.

Four months, supposedly

We started around the summer of 2021, thinking it would take about four months and not cost very much. We made a lot of mistakes.

We built the terrace and then realised we hadn’t put insulation underneath. That would make the place pretty much unusable for a large part of the year. So we took it apart and did it again.

The timber for the frame came from Yura’s new plot, where he and his wife Ksenia wanted to build their own home. These were irregular pieces of wood, not standard lengths from a shop. Making them work together took much more of Mykola’s time. Sourcing locally sounded simple. It was expensive and difficult.

We used reclaimed wood, linen insulation and ocheret, reeds, for roofing and insulation. Everything presented another problem to solve. Even the way the frame met the terrace was unusual: I kept calling the strip around the bottom a skirting board. This was a very inventive, very hands-on build, and the budget just kept growing.

I celebrated my birthday there in late November 2021. It was mostly built. Most of the remaining materials were on site. Work slowed through the winter, but we thought we’d finish the following summer.

Then Russia launched the full-scale invasion. Most of us were displaced. Mykola stayed. The project stopped.

Finishing it meant something else

In April 2023, I went hiking in Nepal with Ksenia. She still visited Balyki and told me how hard things had become for Mykola. There was very little work. The people who had been building this life together were scattered. She encouraged me and Yura to get things moving on our land again, to do something useful for the people still there.

I decided to finish Kurin. Whatever it took.

We made a minimal plan. That went over budget too. Building during the war was terribly difficult, and what we started discussing in spring 2023 didn’t get finished until 2025. Near the end, Mykola joined the Ukrainian armed forces. Other people did the final finishing touches.

We opened in the summer of 2025, had a test season, and then a full season in 2026. People can now come and stay in this thing we spent so long trying to finish.

In October 2026, Kurin Balyky won Hutshub’s “Відкриття року” — Opening of the Year. I’m very proud of that. I’m also very proud that the place exists at all.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

A place I haven’t yet visited in its finished form.

I think I spent eight or ten times what I originally expected. That sounds ridiculous, and it is. But I don’t regret it.

I started this because I wanted a place for myself. Somewhere to get out of the city, sit outside, have a weekend. By the time we restarted it during the war, finishing it had become a way to keep some work and some life going in a community I cared about.

It isn’t making me a lot of money. It has given people work through the building years, and it employs local people now. The people who come to stay bring business into the community, too. Supporting that community is something I try to keep doing through everything connected to this place.

And for people living in Kyiv now, a weekend away means something very different from what I had in mind in 2021. When your everyday life includes bombings and shelling, being able to leave the city for a while, sit outside and have a few quiet days matters enormously. Kurin has become one of the places people can go for that respite. That feels like a very important reason for it to exist.

It also gave me a friendship with Mykola that carries on well beyond the building. Recently, a friend in Britain completed a fundraiser that provided a vehicle for him to use in his service with the Ukrainian armed forces. The donation came from them; I helped connect their support to Mykola. We started by talking about a kurin down at the water source, and we’re still finding ways to be part of each other’s lives.

The strange part is that I still haven’t been there since it was finished. I’ve seen the photographs. Other people have stayed there. It’s won an award. And I’m still hoping I’ll get to go back and spend some time in it myself.
