EEG / virtual reality / 2016

Belka

In 2016, I went to a hackathon to see if we could build a world out of someone else’s brain activity. A few months later, I found myself using it to wind up fintech people.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Русская версия ↗

Hack the Senses, 2016

I found out about a hackathon in London called Hack the Senses and invited my friend Doniyor. It sounded like a fun thing to do. I was already spending a lot of time around VR and reading about the connection between humans and machines, so I brought my HTC Vive and my computer and pitched an idea: could we create a virtual environment that responded to a person’s brain activity? One person would wear an EEG sensor, and another would enter the world it was changing. A small attempt to let someone into another person’s head.

There was a voting system for the pitches, and the people who liked mine became my team. We called ourselves Belka and got to work. Our ambitions were considerable for a weekend. We wanted complex environments generated from the signal, with a soundtrack responding to the person’s state as well. EEG produces a lot of noisy data, and Doniyor managed to turn that into something we could use. The generative music proved too difficult to finish in the time we had. The guy working on it tried very hard, but it never came together.

As the deadline approached, the complex environment became an open world that changed colour. That was our working prototype, the result of the usual last-minute hackathon scramble. I also discovered that putting a sensor on someone and leaving them alone gave the person in the headset very little to look at. I edited a video with contrasting images and music, moving from calming things to irritating ones, and we brewed coffee and brought it over for the smell. Now there was something happening to the person wearing the EEG, and the other person could watch the world respond.

People liked it. We came second and won the Intel Code for Good prize, which felt like a very good outcome for an idea I had brought along for a bit of fun. I was excited about developing it further, but the core team didn’t want to carry on. I don’t remember what happened to the prize money, or even if we ever received it. The invitation to exhibit at a technology conference did arrive, although I promptly forgot about that too.

Very calm people

About two days before The Tech Expo, someone called to ask if we were coming. Of course we were. I printed a banner and showed up with an assistant who had nothing to do with the original team. It was October 2016, and VR was still a novelty. Most of the other VR people seemed to be making virtual previews of expensive apartments for London property developers. There was also a lot of fintech. A fucking lot of fintech.

I remember a fairly small room, perhaps twenty or twenty-five stands, and feeling very much out of place in it. Everyone seemed to have a commercially useful thing to demonstrate, while I had an improvised booth, a colourful scarf and an experiment I was curious about. The fact that I had no particular plans for it would become a source of great confusion throughout the event.

We had the EEG, the Vive and a screen showing the virtual world to everyone standing around. One person could put on the sensor, another could go inside the world, and the rest could watch. I explained the colour scheme: blue was calm; yellow and red were less so. It was simple enough to understand, which turned out to be all the invitation people needed.

Somebody would come over, listen to the explanation and announce that they were very calm. I would offer them the sensor, and the world would promptly go yellow or red. They would try to calm down, which became a little harder now that their friend was watching and laughing. I enjoyed this immensely. In a room full of finance people, telling someone that most visitors found it difficult to be calm was a reliable way of getting them to volunteer.

The scene kept repeating, each time with a slightly bigger audience. First it was a guy and his friend, then a couple of people, and by the last demonstration I remember there were perhaps ten or fifteen gathered around a man in a suit, all watching him try to hold his shit together while the world kept suggesting he wasn’t doing very well.

Everyone wanted to know what I was planning to do with it. I kept responding that I didn’t know, probably nothing, and people seemed genuinely taken aback. I wasn’t being coy. I had no desire to build a startup at the time. We had made something, people were having an interesting experience with it, and that was already quite a lot for me.

“I could, but I won’t.”

One woman came over and asked a lot of questions. Eventually, she wanted to know if I could get a sense of somebody’s emotional state with something less intrusive than a sensor on their head. I had been reading papers and going to talks about this sort of thing, and from what I understood, it seemed possible, especially in a room where you controlled the conditions. I told her so.

She was an executive at Sberbank, and she had a particular use in mind. She described a room where the bank would receive high-net-worth clients. The room would read their emotional state, and the script of the conversation would change in response. That was her pitch. Would I build something like that?

“I could, but I won’t.”

I had left finance a year or two earlier and still had a considerable chip on my shoulder about it. Sberbank belonged to the same government that had fucked us over, which hardly helped. But the idea itself felt wrong to me. Somebody would walk into that room, and their emotional state would become part of the bank’s sales process. I felt that crossed a personal boundary, that it infringed on their freedom. I wanted no part of it.

I was delighted to say no. It felt like such a clear, obvious thing to refuse, and I felt very righteous about refusing it. For years, I told this story with the same satisfaction.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

On winding people up, refusing Sberbank, and changing my mind.

How little it took

What interests me now is how much of the original idea we could abandon and still have something happen between people. We wanted complex environments and generative music. We ended up with a world that changed colour, and that was enough for someone to learn the rules, see a change and respond to the person causing it. I still find that exciting. The people in the experience needed much less information than I had imagined.

Obviously, I don’t take those colours as an infallible reading of anybody’s mind. It was a rough prototype. What stays with me is the experience it made possible: one person looking around a world being changed by someone standing next to them. That still makes me curious about how much further it could go.

I also see the conference as an experiment with hubris, although that part wasn’t planned. There is something very funny about trying to prove how calm you are while a growing crowd watches you fail. And I can hardly pretend I was a neutral observer. I kept giving people reasons to think they could do better than the last guy. Looking back, I can see how much I was enjoying the chance to wind up people from a world I had only recently left.

Ten years later

Today, I would probably take the opportunity. The biggest change is in how I understand the boundary I felt so certain about then. I no longer feel that reading someone’s emotional state is, in itself, an infringement on their freedom.

The more attention I pay to how we respond to one another, the less I believe we hide our emotions as well as we imagine. We sense tension, desire, discomfort and fear in the people around us all the time, often without being able to explain how we know. I notice this much more in my own life now. Using a machine to pick up some of those signals no longer feels like crossing the line it did back then. My understanding of what is already happening between people has changed.

I still don’t particularly like the idea of helping a bank sell more things this way. Don’t get me wrong. But I no longer feel that trying to build it would necessarily mean selling out. I can see how much I might learn from the work, and that prospect is exciting to me. I suspect comparable systems already exist, too, but that isn’t what changes my answer. I see the thing itself differently.

I have no regrets about the refusal. I understand why it felt so right to me then. Yet I would probably give a different answer today, and I have mixed feelings about that.
