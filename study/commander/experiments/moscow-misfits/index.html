Production services / collective / 2018–2019 / selected work

Moscow Misfits

We learned how to get a film made in Russia, built a team around it and thought we might turn that experience into a business. The film was draining my money. It seemed reasonable to try making some on the side.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

A production business

Producing Aquatilis, now The Ocean Within, felt like working in the Wild West. Permissions were difficult, and with foreigners in the crew we couldn't afford to improvise our way around them. A shoot being shut down was a real possibility. We learned how to get things done and built a capable team in Russia.

Moscow Misfits was our attempt to offer that team and experience to other productions. I had contacts in China and America and was well established in London. Justin was in Berlin. We thought we could bring international projects into Russia and provide the local production work.

Our website called us a creative laboratory, and there were plenty of creative ambitions in it. But the practical idea was fairly straightforward: we knew people who might need to shoot things, and we knew people who could help them do it. Ideally, some money would change hands.

A difficult place to sell

The problem was finding productions that wanted to take the risk. Russia had already occupied Crimea and was already at war. We weren't as aware of what that meant for our proposition as the people being asked to commit serious production budgets. Visas, permissions and the lack of a convincing rebate proposition made the offer harder still.

We also worked with a new initiative to promote Moscow as a filming destination. Our experience of completing an international production with the permissions in place seemed to make us a useful example. We discussed being featured as a production-services company, contributed work and spent months trying to help get things going.

Then our contacts stopped replying. After a long silence, we heard accounts of internal political conflict. We never got a satisfactory explanation directly. Whatever the institutional story was, the partnership we had been working towards went nowhere.

FML48 worked

One of our early projects was FML48. We saw filmmakers who weren't getting much support and organised a forty-eight-hour filmmaking competition. With almost no promotional budget, sponsors and a lot of work, it became an event I am still proud of.

Ani and I designed the awards, put together the ceremony and worked on developing a project with the winning team. We pitched it extensively. The interest we found was largely in producing outside Russia, which didn't do much for our plan to bring work into the country. The screenplay was eventually optioned by a Russian producer, but that didn't give us the continuing production role we had hoped for.

The FML48 page has the films, talks and the fuller story of what followed the competition.

The last proposal

FML48 earned us some credibility as organisers. A Russian streaming platform approached us about a nationwide script competition, with the strongest scripts developed into short-form productions. We put together a proposal with a budget and fees that would pay us for making it happen.

The idea met internal resistance to using an outside production company. After a series of conversations, the opportunity became a temporary job for Ani inside the platform. By then I was ready to wind Moscow Misfits down. She had done an enormous amount of work on FML48, and I wanted her to at least get paid.

She received a salary for a few months. Something was eventually produced, but in my assessment it fell well short of the programme we had proposed. Ani was taken off it before it concluded. That was the end of our attempt to make this into a business.

Other collaborations

The work also connected us to Sean Rogg's Waldorf Project. FUTURO-X at Wonderfruit in Thailand in December 2018 and FUTURO-A aboard a Lufthansa flight in March 2019 were both successes in my assessment. They have their own production account, including the earlier Chapter Three attempt.

The FUTURO-X film credits Moscow Misfits with film production. Its making-of gives the individual production roles, including my work as executive producer and drone camera. FUTURO-A credits Justin as producer/camera and me as executive producer.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

The business proposition, the budgets, and what worked anyway.

The business we thought we were building

I thought the difficult part was learning how to produce in Russia. Once we had done that, surely we could offer it to someone else. But knowing how to solve the local problems didn't make the location attractive to the people paying for the production. In hindsight, they understood some of the risks better than I did.

We made Aquatilis with private investors and a willingness to take risks that a conventional large production would have struggled to accept. That was a way of getting our particular film made. It was a poor foundation for assuming we could bring a steady flow of international clients into the same conditions.

What I was being asked to agree to

At around the same time, I was looking for completion money for Aquatilis and talking to Russian funds and financiers. I was offered access to government money on the condition that half of it came back as a kickback. Fifty percent. I never received an offer with a smaller kickback.

Those were the conversations I encountered, and they shaped how I came to understand the environment we were trying to work in. I also came to suspect that keeping control of a budget could matter more than getting the best production made. That was my reading of the resistance to bringing us into the streaming project; I couldn't see every internal conversation.

I had wanted to build a business around doing the work properly. By the end, I had very little interest in trying to fit into the system I was encountering.

A failed business and a good event

Moscow Misfits didn't become what we hoped. It also doesn't make sense to dismiss FML48 because the company around it didn't work out. We brought people together, made an event with very little money and did real development work afterwards. I'm proud of that.

The difference matters to me. A project can work beautifully for the people taking part and still fail to provide the livelihood or continuing business its organisers hoped to build.
