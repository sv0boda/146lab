Participatory art / performance / 2023

Sack the Tyrant

I built a fake protest as a provocation. It became the most powerful artwork I'd made.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

A home away from home

In early 2023, I was in Bali, displaced from Kyiv. Someone invited me into a group organising a local burn called Lampu. I got excited. I wanted to get away from thinking about the war for a while and suggested making a camp with friends from my Ukrainian community.

They asked around and said they wanted nothing to do with it: it was Russian-organised. I hadn't known that. I looked into it and came to the same conclusion.

The theme, as I remember it, was Home. One camp would be a bedroom, another a kitchen, another a playroom. A big cosy home for everyone.

And I thought: what about the reason you left? What about the place where speaking up can get you beaten, whose leader invaded a sovereign state? That was the home I wanted to bring into the festival.

Ministry of Internal Pressure

I gathered a team: people from Shocker, friends in the NFT world, and people who joined through Telegram chats. We chose an unpleasant bit of the grounds, near rubbish containers and toilets, but visible from much of the site.

We put up a small rally tent with Putin's face and quotations from the Russian, Belarusian and Ukrainian constitutions about freedom of expression. I rented a van, dressed it up like Russian police and put blue lights on top. Except the writing said Министерство внутреннего давления: Ministry of Internal Pressure.

People would see the van, then Putin's face, and come over asking what the fuck was happening. We'd offer them a game.

Join the protest

We built a cinema showing a short loop I'd cut from protests in Moscow, Kazakhstan and Belarus. You signed a long waiver, put on an anti-bark collar and joined the people on screen. You shouted with the protesters. Shouting meant getting shocked.

There was a game structure: endure enough shocks and you could symbolically overthrow a tyrant. Ten got you Putin. An NFT recording your result was part of the concept.

People were being filmed, so we offered masks. Some wouldn't enter even with one. They didn't want to say anything political because they were afraid of the consequences.

We stayed in the room with participants, shouted alongside them, encouraged them and supported them. And very quickly, this stopped feeling like the earlier versions.

The mask

One man asked me detailed questions about the experience as a potential study. He went in, kept going and then broke down crying. While I was trying to comfort him, he described it like a dam breaking: grief, pain and emotions he'd stored away, suddenly coming out. That's how I remember him explaining it.

Then a group came through separately. A woman was wearing a mask. The experience was clearly hard for her, but she kept going. In the middle of it, she took off the mask and cried.

We asked why she'd taken it off. She told us she was tired of being afraid. She didn't want to be afraid anymore.

Another participant recited her poetry, crying and continuing through the shocks. I remember her reaching fifteen. I was getting goosebumps over and over, standing in that room with people.

At this moment I find myself in Bali, being displaced from Kyiv by a war. There is a local burn happening, organised by a Russian burners community in which I am revisiting the pain infliction performance technique.

This time it’s called “Sack the Tyrant”. I am planning to invite people into an enclosed immersive space where they’re going to find themselves in the middle of a big political rally. They will be wearing a shock collar and they will be encouraged to express themselves vocally. They will get electrocuted with increasing intensity for their expression and eventually when they decide they’ve had enough be extracted out of the rally into a police van. At that point they’ll get a physical object to commemorate their achievement of overcoming the pain and fear to express themselves as well as an NFT. Depending on the amount of shocks they can endure, they will have dropped a particular tyrant.

Mint Everything — iActivism 5 March 2023 · The original publication note about NFTs, sharing the podcast and supporting the work.

State of meaningful NFT world.

It’s been a few years of big promises on how NFTs are going to revolutionise the royalty world. Today there is an unfathomable amount of digital marketplaces for images, that sell randomly generated shit. There is about fifty music NFT publishing platforms, every single one of them doxxing their artists and most of them requiring artists to pass their curation test before being able to publish their work. I haven’t found one decent video NFT publishing platform, nor a podcast destination.

For text there’s an abundance, but mirror.xyz is doing a great job of keeping it simple.

For now, the problem of ownership and direct relationship with a fan remains unsolved. An artist is forced to use web2 platforms for exposure, not being able to own his content there, nor monetise it directly.

Until someone comes along with a simple way of minting your content, self-hosting and publishing it, I came up with this toolset for my artistic practice.

I am going to publish my stuff on web2 platforms and then mint entries on mirror with links to my content.

That way, if someone wants to support me, they can do so by collecting the mirror.xyz entry.

This is the first one, here I am publishing a link to my podcast.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

A joke that became the most powerful work I had made.

I'd started partly as a joke, trying to interrupt this cosy home-away-from-home idea. I hadn't expected it to become so important to people, or to me.

With Greed Is God, people mostly seemed to be having fun or competing with friends. Shocker taught me how much that competition needed a crowd. At Lampu, people had a fucking beef with tyrants.

In Greed Is God, fewer than 10% of people went all the way. In Sack the Tyrant, 50% reached ten shocks, enough to overthrow Putin in the game. Half the people who took part. That was a very different response.

I experienced it as people crossing an internal boundary around what they felt allowed to say. I had ideas about the throat, the physical feeling of being unable to speak, and what the electricity might be doing. But I can't tell you it released a psychological block. These were people's accounts and my experience of being there, rather than proof of a mechanism or a treatment.

For me, this was the most powerful piece I'd made. You can start with a joke and strike a chord you hadn't realised was there. Suddenly the work matters in a way you didn't plan.

I think it could have been reproduced then, and I wish I'd shown it more widely. Instead, I sat on the footage. There is a film in that material that I still haven't edited. I don't know whether doing the performance again now would mean the same thing. It belonged to a particular moment, and the moment has changed.
