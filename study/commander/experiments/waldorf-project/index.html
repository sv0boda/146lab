Immersive theatre / production / 2016–2019

Waldorf Project

I went to a show that blew my mind and wanted to help take it around the world. We eventually made it to a festival in Thailand and a plane over the Atlantic, although the route was considerably less straightforward than that sounds.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Chapter Three FUTURO-X FUTURO-A

The show that started it

I saw Chapter Three / FUTURO with friends during its original London run. I don't remember how I found it. It was fairly underground, and it became one of the best theatrical experiences I had ever had. Afterwards, I needed to meet the person who had made it.

Someone I knew was volunteering on the project and introduced me to Sean Rogg. I was making Aquatilis, the film that eventually became The Ocean Within, and Sean had worked in film before moving into theatre. I showed him the teaser and told him what we were trying to do. He thought it sounded crazy. We got on.

By the end of 2017, Sean was helping on our Cyprus shoot as first assistant director. It coincided with my birthday, friends and partners came over, and among the conversations was an idea: Michael Chow and I would finance two nights of Chapter Three for potential international partners. Let people see it, fall in love with it and help take it elsewhere.

I had no involvement in making the original Chapter Three. My part began with trying to give it that international life.

Two cold nights

We arranged the showcase at a sound stage outside London. People flew in from Los Angeles and China. We had seen something extraordinary and wanted them to experience it too.

A few days before the show, Sean called to say the space was very cold. We agreed to spend more on heating. It wasn't enough. The room was too large for the equipment we had, and the audience began an already challenging experience feeling physically miserable. More heaters for the second night didn't solve it.

For me, the original show had been mind-opening. What we presented on those two nights felt punishing. The showcase failed, we lost the investment, and the international run we had imagined didn't follow.

We kept looking for a way forward, including a Berlin venue that could support the show by providing space. In May 2018, I saw Sean's Chapter Four preview in Stockholm. It was warm, comfortable and fantastic. That reminded me why I had wanted to do this in the first place. Berlin eventually fell apart, but the conversations led us towards two different productions.

FUTURO-X in Thailand

After the Berlin plans collapsed, Sean and I ended up on a friend's boat in London. I came up with the idea of trying a festival version of his work and emailed Pete at Wonderfruit from the boat. He was interested. Calls followed, and we started putting it together.

FUTURO-X took place at Wonderfruit in Thailand on 16 December 2018. The work moved from an intimate audience to thousands of people in a festival setting. I was very hands-on with this production and worked as executive producer, as well as shooting drone footage.

It worked. After the disappointment of the showcase, we had managed to produce an experience on a much larger scale that I felt was a success.

FUTURO-A in flight

Justin had continued the sponsorship conversations around Berlin. The run didn't happen, but Lufthansa offered a different possibility: could the work take place on an aircraft?

On 7 March 2019, FUTURO-A took place aboard a Lufthansa flight from Frankfurt to Austin for SXSW. Justin produced the work, and I helped as executive producer. I was much less hands-on than I had been in Thailand.

This one worked too. We had taken the work into two settings very different from the theatre where I first encountered it. They also turned out to be the end of my producing involvement with Waldorf.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

The room, trust, and knowing when to stop.

The room is part of the work

What I remember most clearly from the failed showcase is the cold. There was an enormous distance between the experience I thought we were reproducing and the one our guests were having, and part of that distance was the temperature of the room.

The original show asked quite a lot of its audience. I could surrender to it because I was comfortable enough to trust what was happening. At the showcase, people were already cold before the work asked anything of them. We had transported the show without adequately recreating the conditions that let it work.

I had trusted Sean to handle the production while I was occupied with the film. Looking back, I needed to be more involved. Agreeing to buy heaters a few days before opening was a very late point to discover whether the venue could support the experience. The warm Stockholm preview later made the difference feel especially clear to me.

A successful show still has to be repeatable

When we first discussed the idea in Cyprus, my friend Ross warned me about key-person risk. So much depended on Sean Rogg delivering. At the time, that didn't seem like a good enough reason to stop. I had seen him deliver something extraordinary.

After the showcase, Sean insisted that the problem was the audience we had brought, not the temperature. To me, that was a much bigger problem than the cold itself. You can get more heaters. But if we can’t agree that people being fucking freezing affected their experience, how are we going to improve the next production? I had my own mistakes to account for, particularly how little oversight I’d given the showcase. But we needed to be able to look at what happened and change how we worked.

Working with Sean, I came to feel that pain and anxiety were part of his process, rather than things he was trying to resolve. Everything became a struggle. I can see how that might protect a very particular artistic vision: less compromise, less collaboration, something closer to the thing in his head. And sometimes the result was extraordinary. That’s why we kept trying. But it also created a deeply unpleasant atmosphere for the people trying to make it happen. I don’t believe that suffering was making the work better. In my view, it wasn’t doing Sean any good either.

Thailand and the flight showed that we could deliver these experiences. What we couldn’t see was a way to keep delivering them under those conditions. Taking the work to other festivals meant building relationships with teams, venues and sponsors who would actually want to do it again. Unless Sean changed his approach to production, there was no realistic way to scale it. We gave him repeated opportunities to do that, and I saw no willingness to change. He was a grown man; I couldn’t make that decision for him. From what I’ve seen in the years since, that approach hasn’t changed. I still think he can make extraordinary work. But extraordinary work doesn’t make the way you produce it irrelevant.
