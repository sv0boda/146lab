Apartment that I built

After years of moving around, I wanted a home in Kyiv. Then I had to work out what that actually meant, one room at a time.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Around 2018, after I got sober, I realised that not having a home was really fucking with me. I’d been moving around for years. I’d had homes before, but none of them remained. My childhood was split between rental properties, and the places we eventually had in Moscow were no longer ours. There was nowhere I could go back to and say: this is home.

I was deeply in love with Kyiv. I could afford a small flat, and ever since living in Moscow I’d wanted a place in a Constructivist building. Kyiv has a few of those, so that’s where I was looking. When I walked into this one, I fell in love. I made an offer and bought it.

One wall, and then everything else

I had a fairly clear idea of how I wanted the place to work, but there was one part of the layout I couldn’t get my head around. I asked Gesine Thomson, the mother of a friend, to have a look.

Gesine is a big deal. Her work spans large-scale community planning and projects across Europe, Asia, Africa, the Middle East and the US. I’ve always admired the career she built as a woman in a profession so dominated by men. To me, she’d achieved the sort of things an architect dreams of doing. And here I was asking her to help me figure out my small flat.

She put a wall where there had been a door, and suddenly the whole thing made sense.

The bathroom was another matter. I wanted a bath I could actually use, a shower and a comfortable separate toilet. The space was small and awkwardly shaped. I spent hours and hours going through options. A Japanese wooden bathtub? Too expensive. A curved glass wall for the shower? Also too expensive. I researched, drew, changed my mind and started again.

Eventually I brought Gesine a couple of options and explained all the suffering that had gone into them. She gave me a few pointers, helped me settle on the final arrangement, and that is pretty much what got built. Then she told me that the way I’d obsessed over this bathroom was how I needed to obsess over every part of the apartment.

I thought: there is no fucking way. I’m not doing this for every corner of the place.

Of course, in the end, I did.

Making it mine

I changed designers along the way. There were people doing drawings and illustrations, people making things, people whose ideas changed the result. I’m grateful for all of that. But I was the person holding the whole thing together, deciding what belonged and what didn’t. I consider myself the main designer of this apartment, and I don’t think that’s an overstatement. It feels very much like me.

The bicycles have something to do with the place, too. The apartment is near the old Kyiv cycle track. These are Takhions, Soviet racing bikes made in Kharkiv, which I think of as the Soviet answer to Italy. I’ve since sold most of them, but kept the Belarusian national team bike. They weren’t just things to hang on a wall. There was a connection to where I was living.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

I’m really proud of this place. Most of it is still exactly how I want it. But there is one room where I didn’t put enough love in, and that’s the kitchen.

I didn’t really cook at the time, so I didn’t know what I wanted. It’s not a bad kitchen. I love it, actually. But compared with the rest of the apartment, it’s almost a placeholder. I knew how I wanted to bathe, work, sit, sleep. I hadn’t yet worked out how I wanted to cook.

If I get to do it again, if the apartment survives this war, that’s the part I’d seriously rework. There are a few other things I’ve learned from living in it, but those are mostly small adjustments. The kitchen is the one room where I’d start again with a better understanding of myself.
