Participatory art / performance / c. 2022

Shocker

The second version: a high score, an NFT idea, and a lesson in needing a crowd.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Wonderfruit, around 2022

After the full-scale invasion, I was travelling through Asia. For some reason, I had the shock collar with me. So I returned to the idea behind Greed Is God and pitched a new version, very late, to Pete, the founder of Wonderfruit. He said yes.

I'd helped produce Sean Rogg's show there in 2018. Since then, the festival had grown substantially. This was a separate work of my own, called Shocker.

The score

You sat on a table, barked, meowed or made whatever noise you wanted, and got shocked. The idea was a high-score competition, with an NFT recording how many shocks you'd taken. I think it was livestreamed too, but I need to find the material before saying exactly which parts of that setup happened.

The festival placed us on the outskirts, next to a friend's camp. We weren't getting much traffic, and I got frustrated. It was portable, so we went gonzo and moved towards the middle of the festival.

Then we got in trouble. Pete came over and explained that he'd placed us further out deliberately: the work was fairly insane, and the festival had become more family-friendly. That was his point, as I remember the conversation. I hadn't treated the placement as part of that decision.

Maybe five or six people went through it. We stopped quite early. It was fun while it worked, but it wasn't as exciting as I'd hoped, and I never formalised the results.

Related work

Before this came Greed Is God. Afterwards came Sack the Tyrant, where the reason to keep going changed.

Contributions

Concept and performance: Vlad Svoboda, with collaborators whose individual credits are still being gathered.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

Competition needs spectators and a way to follow the scores.

Competition needs a crowd. That was clear to me straight afterwards, and it still is. Where we were placed, there wasn't much possibility of one forming. At one point a little crowd did watch a couple of guys going at it, and you could feel the difference.

I didn't think it needed to be the festival's centre-stage piece. It needed a place where people could stop, watch and draw other people in.

It also needed a proper interactive presentation: something like a television game show, with a visible leaderboard updating after every hit. People needed to be able to follow the rivalry. That was a lesson for a future version, rather than something we'd managed to build there.
