Acting / community / Kyiv and beyond / 2021–2023

Diistvo

We started with an acting course for people who weren’t actors. It became a growing business, a community and probably the most meaningful work I’ve done. Then the war scattered us. We tried to put it back together elsewhere, but we couldn’t put it back together the way it had been.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Before the class

Like quite a few things here, this starts with Ocean Within, when it was still called Aquatilis. We had scientists playing versions of themselves, and they needed help being in front of a camera. That was how I met Vasily Shevchenko — Vasya.

We didn’t have much money. The first proposition was essentially: come to this incredible place, we’ll cover your expenses and look after you, but the fee will be very small. If it works, there’ll be a proper job on the larger production. He came. He did a fantastic job. The people he coached loved it, the director loved it, and I knew I had met someone who could get complete beginners to do something worthwhile as actors. I also liked that he had been willing to take a chance on the project before there was much in it for him.

My own route into acting was different. Around that period, an actress I knew brought me to a class with Tom Radcliffe, who teaches Meisner technique. I watched people respond to each other in ways I hadn’t thought possible. It looked like they were reading minds. I couldn’t stop thinking about it.

I became sober in June 2018. The programme helped me understand addiction and stop drinking. Therapy helped me deal with the social anxiety underneath it. Both mattered enormously. But I also missed a version of myself: drunk, I could be warm, outgoing, expressive. Sober, making contact was harder. I had grown up with the idea that a man showing emotion was showing weakness. You wore a poker face until you forgot you were wearing it.

Eventually I went to an intensive with Tom in Italy. It changed my life. It was also extremely difficult. I was the non-actor in a room full of professional actors, feeling as though they had an emotional range I could barely touch. Some of the work brought things up that I didn’t know how to deal with. I kept returning, and I’ll tell that story properly elsewhere. For this one, what matters is that acting had become something I wanted other people to experience too.

A very ugly certificate

After moving to Kyiv, I couldn’t shut up about it. Friends kept telling me to organise a class.

Around late 2020, my friend Marina Petrova decided to give her partner, Egor Petrov, a birthday present: a certificate for my acting course, whenever I got around to making it happen. No pressure. Just make the certificate.

Egor was a web designer, so naturally I made the ugliest certificate I could, using WordArt. Then I forgot about it.

A little later, Lyza moved to Kyiv. We met, talked, and I asked whether she wanted to help produce the course. I would handle sales and the main organising work; she would handle the production side — finding a space, travel, tickets, all the things that let an event actually happen. We would split our share equally. Vasya was interested too, and we agreed that half of what we made would go to him as the teacher, with the other half shared between us.

The first course happened around the summer of 2021. Eleven or twelve people, a beautiful space, a really good group. We lost a little money. The arrangement was to share what we earned, but I still paid Vasya a small amount because I didn’t want his time to go completely unpaid. He was understanding about it.

Then the people who had been there couldn’t shut up about it either.

Something that was actually working

We organised another round in the autumn. It was bigger, sold out and went wonderfully. People brought friends. People with an audience talked about us. By the next round, in January and February 2022, we were trying different formats: the regular course, a professional group, a shorter class focused on speech and the body.

We also ran a corporate course for Banda, which I think of as the best agency in Ukraine. Their team’s feedback is still here.

The point wasn’t that everyone needed to become an actor. People came into a room and practised listening, responding, paying attention to their bodies, expressing something instead of immediately shutting it down. There was playfulness. There was contact. You could try something, look ridiculous and find that the world hadn’t ended.

Reading the participant accounts now, I recognise what excited me then. People describe learning to let go of control, to slow down, to notice one another. They describe friendships continuing after the course. Those were their experiences, and they were why I wanted to keep doing it.

And it was working as a business. We were making good money, growing quickly and enjoying the work. That combination matters. I’ve had plenty of projects where the experience was wonderful and the finances were a disaster. Here, for a while, both sides worked. People were raving. I felt like we were doing God’s work, which is a fairly grand thing to say about an acting class, but that is honestly how it felt.

I took part in almost every course myself.

Less than two weeks later

As I remember it, the last classes finished less than two weeks before the full-scale invasion.

Afterwards, I received messages from people saying they didn’t know how they would have got through the first months without that experience. The course had given them strength, connection, something to hold on to. I hadn’t organised it to prepare anyone for a war. Hearing that from people was overwhelming.

Then the community we had built was scattered.

Lyza, Annette and I left Ukraine together. Annette had been working on our social media. We were very much a work family by then, and they were a huge part of my being able to leave safely as a Russian citizen. Lyza stayed in Warsaw. Everyone was trying to work out what their life would be now.

Vasya went quiet. When we eventually spoke, I challenged him about not taking a clear public position on the war. He said he had posted, but what I’d seen was general language. I asked whether he was afraid to say “war.” He said he wasn’t. To me, he seemed afraid. It was the beginning of a disagreement that we never really resolved.

Trying again elsewhere

We ran a charitable workshop for Ukrainian refugee children in Warsaw with Future for Ukraine. That went well. It wasn’t a commercial project, but it was work that felt worth doing.

I tried to organise a course in Spain. We didn’t sell the places, and it didn’t happen. The announcements survive; the class doesn’t.

When we were nearly ready to give up, Vasya invited us to Istanbul, where he was living. Lyza, another friend and I spent a few days practising with him and some of the actors he was coaching. We decided to try again in Bali.

We ran two courses there, along with some smaller events. The classes themselves went really well. We had Russians and Ukrainians in roughly equal numbers, and for people who took part, there was something important in being able to see each other as human beings. I experienced those rooms as healing. But a good experience in the room didn’t make the project viable outside it.

Selling it was painfully difficult. In the conversations we were having, Russians hesitated because we had a Ukrainian name and a history of Ukrainian communication. Ukrainians hesitated because the coach was Russian. Vasya still hadn’t clearly voiced his position. He was trying to be diplomatic at a moment when, to me, that simply wasn’t a workable position between these communities.

Lyza had moved to London and needed to build her own life. She couldn’t be as involved. I was supposed to be concentrating on my book, but instead I was doing a lot of the organising in Bali and losing money. Vasya was unhappy about what he was earning. We both needed something to live on, but I couldn’t manufacture more sales out of the situation we were in.

At the same time, the work he wanted to do was moving towards shamanic practices. I financed a space for him at Burning Man, intending it to offer people a taste of the course and help us build the next one. He decided instead to use it for his shamanic work. That was his choice, but it wasn’t what I thought I was helping produce. We were increasingly working towards different things.

Mount Agung

The final break came with a climb.

Vasya had already climbed Agung and proposed taking a group up for a heart-opening practice around Valentine’s Day. I gathered about fifteen people and helped organise it. He was supposed to provide the practical knowledge and arrange the local support. I had no mountaineering experience then, so I relied on someone who had already done the ascent to tell us what we needed.

The advice, as I remember it, amounted to: no flip-flops, wear sneakers, bring snacks and water. Nothing special.

We started around one in the morning, aiming to reach the summit around sunrise. It began raining early in the night, and the climb was brutal. By the time we got to the top, we had spent something like four hours in the rain. We were soaking wet, without proper layers, and fucking freezing. It was still pouring. There was no sunrise. There was no heart-opening practice.

There was a woman having a panic attack at the top of the mountain. She was a yoga instructor, a strong person, and she was overwhelmed. I was trying to comfort her and asking what we should do. This was the situation we had brought people into. They had signed up for this beautiful, meaningful experience, and here we were, drenched and shivering on a mountain, with someone in real distress and very little support.

In the end, we had some stale tea, and Vasya showed us how to jump and breathe rapidly to warm ourselves up. That was what the promised experience had come to. The gap between what we had offered people and what we actually delivered was horrible. I had gathered these people. They had trusted us. I felt responsible for them, and I had relied on him for the knowledge that was supposed to make this work.

Everyone reached the top, and everyone got down safely. I’m grateful for that. But getting everyone home did not turn this into a good experience, or make the way we had prepared for it acceptable. It was a massive fuck-up. By the time we got down, I knew I couldn’t work with him anymore.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

The class I still miss, and why we couldn’t keep rebuilding it.

The class I still miss

I think basic acting training belongs in far more people’s lives than it does. Not because everyone needs a stage career. Because recognising an emotion, allowing yourself to express it and communicating it to another person are useful human abilities, and many of us never had much opportunity to practise them.

For me, that was the heart of Diistvo. It was an acting class, but the thing I wanted from it went beyond acting. I wanted people to have more freedom in how they connected. More ways to express themselves. More playfulness. We had professional actors taking the basic classes too, and they still got something from them. You don’t finish learning how to pay attention to another person.

I’ve run a couple of circles for friends since then. They went well. I don’t think they worked as well as the courses with Vasya, and I don’t pretend I can replace his experience. He is a very capable teacher. That is part of why this story is difficult, rather than a simple explanation of why someone was bad and we stopped working together.

What I could no longer accept

By Agung, the political disagreement had become part of a larger question about values and responsibility. I had gathered people for an experience and relied on him for knowledge I didn’t have. I needed preparation, support and a shared understanding of what we were asking those people to do. I didn’t feel we had that.

My own classes with Tom had shown me how powerful this kind of work could be, and how difficult. I knew what it was like to uncover more than you could comfortably handle. That made the conditions around the experience matter to me. The teacher’s skill was important, but it couldn’t be the whole answer.

Looking back, I think the partnership would eventually have reached a breaking point even without the war. There was a deep difference in how we understood the work and what it required of us. That doesn’t erase what Vasya gave people, or what we made together. It explains why I couldn’t keep trying to rebuild it with him.

What the war took

The war killed the project that was working in Kyiv. The later attempts also showed me that recreating it would take more than gathering the same people in another country. Both things are true.

I’ve tried to revive it a few times. So far, those attempts haven’t become anything sustained. I don’t currently want to become the teacher myself, but I also know that the thing I want to build doesn’t quite match what most acting coaches understand as their job. I still need to find a way to articulate it, and people who want to make it happen with me.

Of everything I’ve done, this remains the most meaningful to me, and the one most obviously destroyed by the war. I miss the classes myself. I don’t just miss running them. I miss being in them.

I hope there is another version ahead.
