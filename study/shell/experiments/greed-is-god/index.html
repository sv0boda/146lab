Greed Is God

A business centre in an outhouse. A job that hurts. A fake Rolex when you retire. My first solo artwork.

↓ Looking back — what I think about it now

Open on YouTube ↗

Bombay Beach Estates

In 2018, I was at the Bombay Beach Biennale helping build other people's artwork, especially my friend Stefan Ashkenazy's. There was a team of us. He was the artist; I was one of the people figuring out how to make things happen. The following year, I wanted to make something myself.

Bombay Beach sits on the Salton Sea. The Biennale makes work among its abandoned buildings, vacant lots and deteriorating shoreline. It is also a place where people live. Stefan, one of the festival's founders, had a plot called Bombay Beach Estates. Stucco posts, gold chain, a beautiful gate and a lovely sign. Inside: dilapidated shacks.

In the middle was a broken outhouse. And I thought, if you've got Bombay Beach Estates, you've got to have a business centre.

Gitane Tower

Glass wall, AstroTurf, fax machine, computer screen. All the things that make an outhouse a serious place of business. I called it Gitane Tower, after Stefan's community, Cirque Gitane.

Stefan didn't love it. I kept bothering him for a few days, and eventually he agreed. I paid for most of it; he supplied some materials. I arrived about a week before the event and started building. I had very little experience actually building things, but I built a business centre. That part was wonderful.

As I was doing it, I realised there had to be a performance inside. I was throwing ideas back and forth with Sean Rogg. We'd been working together on other projects, and some fairly stupid ideas involving electric shocks came out of those conversations. Eventually, I came up with the job.

Employment and retirement

I hired people to sit in the business centre wearing an anti-bark collar. The kind sold for dogs. They barked, the collar shocked them, and the intensity increased with successive shocks.

First, they signed an incredibly stupid waiver. I don't have the exact wording in front of me, but essentially it said: if I die, or get hurt physically or emotionally, that's my problem. And I'm being filmed so other people can laugh at me later. That was the tone of the employment contract.

The first level felt like almost nothing to me. By four, it was incredibly painful. There were seven levels. The retirement rules changed during the night: I remember starting at seven shocks, then making it ten. The retirement gift was a Rolex.

A fake one, obviously. I'd sourced eight in L.A. and engraved Greed Is God into their crystals with a Dremel. We gave all eight away on the first and only night. I'd considered running it for longer, but we'd exhausted the retirement fund.

The film

The film above documents that night. It gives you the experience with very little explanation. I made it as a diploma project for an editing class, with mentorship from Alex Chorny.

Related work

The idea returned in Shocker at Wonderfruit, then changed again in Sack the Tyrant.

Looking backWhat worked, what didn’t, and what changed.

Becoming an artist, and the things people did for fun.

When I started, I called it an art piece almost as a joke. I was playful about it. Then it became an artwork in the full sense: an idea I was trying to explore, completely unhinged, with an imperfect attempt to get some science into it. I asked participants about their motivations afterwards and tried to formalise it as a study. I need to find those questionnaires; what follows is my recollection.

The biggest result was that I felt I'd become an artist. This was my first solo piece, and it worked.

That doesn't mean the idea arrived in a vacuum. My best ideas usually happen when I'm throwing things back and forth with other people. I'm happy to credit Sean as an influence and Stefan as my artistic godfather. Years of working with Stefan had given me the confidence to try this myself. I don't buy the absolute separation between your ideas and all the people and knowledge that came before you. But I can recognise those influences and still say: this was my work.

Stefan tried it himself and kept going beyond what I remember anyone else enduring. I lost count. When he asked how many, I said something like twelve or thirteen. His response, as I remember it: “Are you fucking counting or are you arbitraging? What the fuck, man? Like, I'm getting hit here.”

I can't give you a reliable final count. At the thank-you party, though, he spoke at length about it. He said he'd initially expected something horrible, as he often did when friends proposed making art. Then he heard people talking about it, tried it and found it the most insane thing he'd done at the festival. He was proud of me. Recognition from someone whose work I aspired to meant an enormous amount.

I also learned that people will do fucked-up things for fun. Most of those who got a watch seemed to be enjoying themselves or proving a point to someone. A lot were friends competing: he'd got a Rolex, so they were fucking getting one too. I don't think greed was really the driver. Maybe the watches being fake mattered. I don't know.

The response I hadn't anticipated came from people who tried it and stopped early. Several told me they worked with animals, used electric shocks and, after feeling this themselves, would never do that again. I can't tell you what they did afterwards. But that's what they told me, and it felt like something positive had come out of this ridiculous thing.

At the time, I wasn't thinking about repeating it. I was thinking about buying land at Bombay Beach, maybe building a Church of Business. Mostly, I felt very accomplished with the thing I'd just made.
